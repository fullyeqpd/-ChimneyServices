## Chimney cap basics: rain, animals, embers, downdraft {#what-it-does}
TAGS: NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS, NATIONAL · IRC
PRICE: cap-single-flue
A chimney cap is a metal hood on legs, wrapped in wire mesh, that sits over the top of a flue. Its first job is keeping rain from falling straight down the flue, where water rusts dampers and softens mortar. The mesh keeps out birds, squirrels and raccoons, and the hood keeps out leaves. A mesh screen that meets the spark arrestor rules in the International Residential Code also catches some burning embers before they land on a roof. Manufacturers such as HY-C also market caps to reduce wind-driven downdraft that pushes smoke back into a room. The cost of skipping a cap usually shows up slowly, as water damage or an animal removal call. Honest caveat: a cap protects the flue opening only, and it does nothing for a cracked crown, failed flashing or porous brick below it (see [the crown guide](/learn/chimney-crown)).

## Cap types: single-flue, multi-flue, full-coverage {#cap-types}
TAGS: NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
PRICE: cap-single-flue, cap-multi-flue, cap-full-coverage
Caps come in three broad shapes. A **single-flue cap** covers one flue tile and clamps or screws directly to that tile. A **multi-flue cap** covers two or more flues under one hood and is fastened to the crown around them. A **full-coverage** or outside-mount cap covers the entire top of the chimney, crown included, and attaches to the outside of the masonry. HY-C says four stock sizes fit most single flues, but the tile's shape and how far it sticks up above the crown both matter. Multi-flue and full-coverage caps are usually measured and often custom made, which is why their researched ranges run wider than single-flue caps. Honest caveat: a full-coverage cap shelters the crown, but it does not repair one that is already cracked, so fix the crown first.

## Cap materials: galvanized, stainless steel, copper {#materials}
TAGS: NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
PRICE: cap-single-flue
Most caps are galvanized steel, stainless steel or copper. Galvanized caps cost least but rust over time; HY-C notes that black galvanized steel corrodes faster within about 25 miles of an ocean or salt water. Stainless steel is the common long-life choice, and HY-C says stainless caps often last decades. Copper is chosen mostly for appearance, and HY-C says it can cost double or triple the price of a comparable stainless cap because the metal itself is scarcer. The consequence of choosing the cheapest metal is usually a second cap purchase and another roof trip later. Ask what grade of stainless is used and what the warranty covers, since terms differ by product and installer. Honest caveat: the material claims here come from a manufacturer that sells all three metals, and we have not independently tested cap lifespan.

## Spark arrestor mesh: what the code says {#spark-arrestor}
TAGS: NATIONAL · IRC, VARIES BY STATE
PRICE: cap-single-flue
The mesh on a cap is what makes it a spark arrestor. The International Residential Code has a section titled Spark arrestors (R1003.9.2) that applies where one is installed on a masonry chimney. A home inspector's summary of that section gives the screen openings as no smaller than 3/8 inch and no larger than 1/2 inch. Mesh that is too fine clogs with soot and creosote and can choke the draft; mesh that is too coarse lets larger embers through. HY-C notes that some jurisdictions set their own mesh rules, so the right screen varies by state and sometimes by city. A clogged screen is a real consequence, not a cosmetic one, because it restricts the flue on a wood-burning appliance. Honest caveat: mesh reduces embers but does not make a roof fireproof, and it still needs to be checked for soot buildup.

## Chase cover vs cap on factory-built chimneys {#chase-cover}
TAGS: APPLIES: FACTORY-BUILT SYSTEMS, NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
PRICE: chase-cover
If your chimney is a wood- or siding-clad box rather than brick, it is probably a factory-built system, and its top is a **chase cover**, not a crown. HY-C describes a chase cover as a pan-like metal sheet with a hole cut for each flue pipe, with a cap sitting on the pipe above it. The cover sheds water off the whole chase, and the cap covers the pipe opening, so the two do different jobs. Galvanneal covers eventually rust, while stainless steel resists corrosion much longer. HY-C warns that a rusty chase cover can crack and let rainwater into the chase, where it rots framing and rusts the firebox. Replacing a failed cover is usually cheaper than repairing the water damage behind it. Honest caveat: factory-built chimneys must follow their own manufacturer's listing, so a replacement cap or cover should match what that listing allows.

## Animals, chimney swifts and caps {#animals}
TAGS: NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
PRICE: animal-removal
A missing or torn cap is the usual way raccoons, squirrels and birds get into a flue. Scratching or chirping in the chimney, nesting material in the firebox, or a strong odor are the common signs. Chimney swifts are a special case: the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service says harassing or harming swifts, their eggs or their nests is illegal under the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act. The agency advises doing chimney inspections and repairs only in the months when swifts are away, typically fall through early spring. It also says a chimney with a metal liner should be capped so birds are not trapped inside. Removal plus exclusion work costs more than the cap that would have prevented it. Honest caveat: the agency's swift guidance advises against capping a chimney just to keep out nuisance wildlife unless it has a metal liner, so capping is a choice, not a universal rule.

## Failed cap signs: when to replace, not repair {#replace-or-repair}
TAGS: NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
PRICE: cap-single-flue, cap-multi-flue, chase-cover
From the ground, look for a cap that is leaning, missing, bent or streaked with rust. Inside, water in the firebox after rain, a rusty damper or animal debris all point to the top of the chimney. HY-C advises replacing covers that are rusty, loose or otherwise damaged, because the water damage that follows costs more than the part. Caps are inexpensive enough that patching rusted mesh or a split hood rarely makes sense; a loose but sound cap can often just be refastened. Ask the company to photograph the cap before and after the work so you can see what was wrong. Honest caveat: water in the firebox has several possible causes, including the crown, flashing and brick, so a new cap alone may not stop a leak.

## DIY cap installation vs hiring out {#diy-vs-pro}
TAGS: NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
PRICE: cap-single-flue
A single-flue cap is one of the few chimney parts a homeowner can physically install, since many clamp or screw onto the tile. The hard part is the roof: HY-C, which sells caps directly, still recommends against climbing a roof to install one yourself because falls cause severe injuries. Measuring matters too, because the flue's shape and how far the tile projects above the crown determine which cap fits. A company already on the roof can also look at the crown, flashing and flue at the same visit. If you hire it out, the FTC advises getting a written estimate that lists the work, the materials and the price. Ask which metal is quoted and whether a single-flue cap is enough before agreeing to a larger multi-flue or full-coverage cap. Honest caveat: a larger cap is sometimes the right call, for example when several flues share one crown, so ask why rather than assume.
