## Creosote basics: what it is and why it burns {#what-it-is}
TAGS: APPLIES: WOOD-BURNING, NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
Creosote is the residue that forms when wood smoke, tar and organic vapors cool and condense on the inside of a chimney. Wood appliance manuals describe it the same way: slow burning produces tar and vapors that condense in cooler flues. CSIA describes creosote as black or brown, and says it can be crusty and flaky, tar-like and sticky, or shiny and hardened, sometimes all in the same chimney. Every form is combustible. NFPA 211, the standard for chimneys and solid-fuel appliances, is the basis for the annual inspection that checks for these deposits. The consequence of letting creosote build is a chimney fire, which can crack flue liners and damage the house even when nobody notices it happening. The caveat: some soot and creosote in a wood-burning chimney is normal, and finding deposits is not by itself a sign that you burned wrong.

## The three stages of creosote and what each looks like {#three-stages}
TAGS: APPLIES: WOOD-BURNING
The trade commonly sorts creosote into three stages, also called degrees. **First degree** is mostly soot: dry, dusty, and removed with a standard chimney brush. **Second degree** looks like shiny black flakes with hardened tar in them, and it does not brush away easily. **Third degree** is glaze: a concentrated, tar-like coating that can run down the flue and harden into a shiny layer. These stages are a trade convention rather than a numbered definition in a building code, so the standard behind the work is still NFPA 211's inspection and cleaning expectations. Cost rises with each stage because the removal method changes. The caveat: stage labels are a visual judgment, and two sweeps can describe the same deposit differently, so ask to see photos of what they are calling glaze.

## Why creosote glaze forms {#why-glaze-forms}
TAGS: APPLIES: WOOD-BURNING
Glaze forms when smoke moves slowly and cools before it leaves the chimney. CSIA names three main causes: restricted air supply, such as closed glass doors or a damper not opened far enough; unseasoned wood, which burns cooler because the fire spends energy boiling off water; and cool chimney temperatures that increase how long smoke lingers in the flue. Overloading a wood stove to burn slowly overnight has the same effect. An oversized flue and a chimney on a cold exterior wall also keep flue temperatures low. Manufacturer manuals and NFPA 211 exist partly to keep appliances, flues and burn practices matched so exhaust stays hot. The consequence is a hard deposit that a brush cannot remove and that burns intensely if it ignites. The caveat: glaze usually has more than one cause, so fixing only the wood without fixing the air supply or flue size may not stop it.

## Creosote removal methods by stage {#removal}
TAGS: APPLIES: WOOD-BURNING, NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
PRICE: chimney-sweep-flat, creosote-deglazing
The removal method follows the stage. First-degree soot is brushed out during a normal sweep. Second-degree deposits are commonly removed with rotary tools that spin whips or brushes against the flue walls. Third-degree glaze may need rotary chain tools or a chemical treatment. CSIA's homeowner guidance says gummy creosote is about the only case where a chemical or acid application is the practical option. One such product, a poultice creosote remover, is labeled for glazed creosote on tile or stainless liners, dries over a day or two, and is then swept out; its label requires eye protection, a respirator and gloves. A standard sweep has the researched national range shown below; deglazing is typically quote after inspection because the amount of glaze varies. The caveat: aggressive mechanical removal can damage a weak liner, so ask how the method fits your flue's condition.

## Chimney fire signs: during and after {#chimney-fire-signs}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211, APPLIES: WOOD-BURNING
PRICE: level-2-inspection
CSIA describes a fast-burning chimney fire as loud cracking and popping, dense smoke, an intense hot smell, and sometimes a low rumble like a freight train. CSIA also says most chimney fires go undetected, because slow-burning ones can reach very high temperatures without drama. Signs of a past fire include puffy or honeycombed creosote, warped metal on the damper or connector pipe, cracked or collapsed flue tiles, a discolored rain cap, creosote flakes on the roof or ground, and cracks in exterior masonry. After a suspected fire, CSIA's Level 2 procedure lists fire damage as a reason for a Level 2 inspection with a camera scan. Do not use the fireplace until it has been checked. The consequence of using a fire-damaged flue is a second fire with less protection than the first. The caveat: some of these signs have other causes, which is why an inspection, not a checklist, is how damage is confirmed.

## Seasoned wood: the moisture rule that limits creosote {#seasoned-wood}
TAGS: APPLIES: WOOD-BURNING
Wet wood is one of the main drivers of creosote, and the fix is measurable. EPA's Burn Wise guidance says wood burns best at a moisture content under 20 percent, recommends seasoning wood for at least six months, and suggests testing it with a wood moisture meter. One wood insert manufacturer says wood between 15 and 20 percent moisture lets its insert reach its highest efficiency. Wet wood wastes heat turning water into steam, which cools the smoke and lets creosote condense. EPA also warns against burning trash, painted or treated wood, driftwood, and wet or moldy wood. The consequence of burning wet wood is more smoke, less heat, and faster buildup that costs more to remove. The caveat: "seasoned" on a firewood ad is not a measurement, so a meter reading on a freshly split face is the only way to know.

## Hot fires and a right-sized flue {#hot-fires-flue-size}
TAGS: APPLIES: WOOD-BURNING, NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
Beyond dry wood, two things keep exhaust hot: how you burn and the flue it travels through. EPA advises building hot fires and says a smoldering fire is neither safe nor efficient for most appliances. CSIA points to restricted air, closed doors and a partly open damper as causes of buildup. The flue matters too: liner and appliance manuals require the flue to match the appliance, and a flue much larger than needed lets smoke slow and cool. NFPA 211 and the appliance's installation manual together set those sizing expectations. The consequence of a mismatch is recurring creosote even with good wood and good habits. The caveat: fixing an oversized flue usually means relining, which is a real cost, so confirm the sizing problem with measurements before treating it as the cause. ## Creosote sweeping logs are not a chimney sweep {#creosote-logs}
TAGS: APPLIES: WOOD-BURNING, NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: chimney-sweep-flat, level-1-inspection
Creosote sweeping logs are sold as a treatment you burn to loosen deposits. The maker of one widely sold log says its chemical action makes creosote brittle so heat breaks it off the flue walls. The same maker answers "No" when asked whether the log replaces professional service, saying it will not tell you if the chimney is blocked or damaged and that a professional should inspect it regularly. CSIA's homeowner guidance takes a similar view: chemical cleaners are not bad in themselves, but using them in place of inspection and cleaning is. NFPA 211's annual inspection covers soundness, deposits and clearances, which no log can check. The consequence of relying on logs alone is that blockages and liner damage go unseen. A [sweep](/learn/chimney-sweeping) removes deposits, and an [inspection](/learn/chimney-inspection) is what checks the structure. The caveat: a log used between professional visits may help with light buildup, and nothing here says it is useless.
