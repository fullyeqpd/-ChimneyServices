## Level 1: the annual check of what's in reach {#level-1}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: level-1-inspection
A Level 1 inspection looks at the readily accessible parts of the chimney, the appliance and the connection between them. "Readily accessible" means parts that can be reached quickly, without tools or ladders. NFPA 211 defines this level, and the CSIA's written procedure says it fits routine cleanings, annual inspections, and swapping an appliance for a similar one. It is meant for a chimney that will keep being used the way it has been, with no big changes. It costs the least of the three levels, and it is usually done during the same visit as a sweep. The inspector is also looking for obstructions, heavy deposits and damage that can be seen inside the firebox and flue. The honest caveat: a Level 1 does not include a camera scan of the full flue. So a clean Level 1 report does not prove there are no cracks higher up.

## Level 2: sale, system change, or after an event {#level-2}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: level-2-inspection
A Level 2 inspection includes everything in a Level 1, plus a video scan of the flue and the accessible attic, basement, crawl space and roof. The CSIA procedure for this level lists its triggers. They include adding or replacing an appliance with a different kind, planning a reline, selling the property, and any building or chimney fire, storm or earthquake. The scan is required at this level. If the camera can't get through because of the flue's size or its bends, the inspector must write that on the report. The inspector also checks clearances, such as insulation or wiring touching the chimney where it passes through floors and the roof. It costs more than a Level 1 because it takes longer and needs the camera. The caveat: a Level 2 only sees what is accessible, so problems behind finished walls can still be missed.

## Level 3: when concealed damage is suspected {#level-3}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: level-3-inspection
A Level 3 inspection reaches concealed parts of the chimney. That can mean removing parts of the building or the chimney structure, such as sections of a wall, a chase or a crown. NFPA 211 reserves this level for when a Level 1 or Level 2 finds signs of a hidden hazard that can't be judged any other way. A chimney fire that may have damaged hidden parts of the system is one example. The CSIA's post-fire guidance explains that the chimney should be swept and scanned before anyone decides it is safe to use again. Because the scope depends on what is opened up, there is no flat price. Expect a quote after inspection, and restoring the opened area is often a separate charge. The caveat: a Level 3 is rarely the right first step. If someone proposes one without a written Level 2 finding behind it, ask them why.

## How often: once a year, even if you rarely burn {#how-often}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: level-1-inspection
NFPA 211 says chimneys, fireplaces and vents should be inspected at least once a year for soundness, deposits and correct clearances. The U.S. Fire Administration tells homeowners to have heating equipment and chimneys cleaned and inspected every year by a professional. The EPA gives the same advice for wood-burning appliances, chimneys and vents. A yearly inspection is what catches a bird's nest, a cracked flue tile or heavy creosote before the next burn season. Inspection and sweeping are different things. Your chimney may pass inspection and not need a sweep that year. If you skip years, the cost is not the fee you saved. It is not knowing whether the flue is safe the next time you light a fire. The caveat: annual is a minimum, not a ceiling. If you burn heavily or use unseasoned wood, deposits can build up faster. See [creosote](/learn/creosote).

## Camera scans: what the video can and can't see {#camera-scan}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: level-2-inspection
A camera scan pushes a small video camera up or down the flue on rods so the inspector can see the liner from inside. The CSIA's Level 2 procedure requires a scan, and its Level 1 procedure treats one as optional. The CSIA's post-fire guidance describes the same method for checking for cracks and failed joints after a chimney fire. A scan is the main reason a Level 2 costs more than a Level 1. Ask for the footage or still images, dated and tied to your address. The CSIA procedure tells inspectors to label photos from the scan with the job's date and location. A flue coated in soot or glaze can hide cracks, so the flue may need to be swept before the scan means much. The caveat: some flues have bends or sizes the camera can't pass. When that happens, the report should say so rather than imply the whole flue was seen. See [flue liners](/learn/flue-liners).

## What a written inspection report should show {#written-report}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
A useful report names the inspection level, the date, and the appliance and flue that were inspected. It should list what was found, with photos or scan stills. It should also give recommendations tied to specific findings. The CSIA's procedures tell inspectors to give the client a copy of the report and to ask the client to sign it. They also say to note deposits and damaged parts and to keep reports on file permanently. The National Chimney Sweep Guild tells homeowners to expect a written report and photos after an inspection. A report like this costs the company very little extra, and it becomes your record for insurance claims, home sales and future comparisons. A verbal "it looks bad" is not a finding. The caveat: a report's format is not set by law, so reports vary a lot between companies. If yours leaves out the level or the photos, ask for them before you pay for any repair.

## Show me what you found: photos of your own chimney {#show-me}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
You can't see inside your own flue or easily get onto your roof, so the photo is your evidence. Connecticut's consumer protection agency advises that photos used to justify work should include something that identifies them as your home. It also says to ask the company to show you exactly where a liner is broken, or to get a second opinion. The CSIA's inspection procedure tells inspectors to label scan photos with the date and location. Reasonable asks: a wide shot that shows your roofline or house next to each close-up, and the scan played back for you on screen. A company doing honest work gives up nothing by showing you. A company that won't show you is asking you to pay on trust. The caveat: a real problem can still be hard to photograph, such as a gap behind a smoke chamber. If a photo can't settle it, a second inspection can. Neither is an insult to the first inspector.

## Recommended vs required: reading the findings {#recommended-vs-required}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211, VARIES BY STATE
Inspection reports tend to mix two kinds of items, and the report should keep them apart. The first kind is a condition that breaks the standard or the appliance maker's instructions and makes the system unsafe to use. A missing liner section, combustibles too close to the flue, or a blockage all fall in this group. The second kind is an upgrade or preventive repair that is sensible but optional, such as a new cap, a waterproofing treatment or a crown sealant. NFPA 211 and the manufacturer's installation instructions are the yardsticks for the first kind. Asking "which standard or instruction does this violate?" sorts most items quickly. The cost of mixing them up is real: optional work sold as urgent, or urgent work put off because it looked optional. The FTC advises getting written estimates that describe the work and never signing under pressure. The caveat: whether a local code or a home sale makes any item mandatory varies by state.

## When a second opinion is worth the fee {#second-opinion}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: level-2-inspection
A second inspection is worth paying for when the recommended repair costs many times more than an inspection. It is also worth it when the finding is hidden inside the flue, or when you felt rushed. The classic cases are "you need a new liner" and "your crown is cracked." Connecticut's consumer protection agency says to get another opinion if the words carbon monoxide or house fire are used in a way that feels alarming. The FTC advises getting several estimates and asking why they differ a lot. For the second opinion, choose a different company. Ask for the same inspection level and give them no hint of the first diagnosis, so they aren't simply confirming it. Credentials to ask about, in order: NCSG company membership and NCSG individual certification, NFI certification for your fuel type, then CSIA certification. The caveat: two honest inspectors can disagree on how urgent something is. Compare the photos and the cited standard, not just the conclusions.
