## What a sweep visit includes, step by step {#what-happens}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: chimney-sweep-flat
A standard visit follows a set order. The CSIA's procedure for a routine Level 1 inspection with sweeping goes like this. The technician looks at the appliance and the chimney first. They set up a drop cloth and bring in equipment, and may run a camera. They check the connector and wall pass-through, then sweep the flue. If the sweeping is done from above, they examine the top of the chimney. Last, they go over the findings with you and give you a report. The same procedure says to note the amount and type of deposits and any signs of an earlier chimney fire before sweeping. Most companies price this as a flat job, sometimes bundled with the Level 1 inspection. Ask which is included before the visit. The caveat: companies vary, and nothing sets out one legally required sweep procedure. If the visit skips the inspection or the report, you got a cleaning, not a service call.

## How often: inspect yearly, sweep at 1/8 inch {#how-often}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211, APPLIES: WOOD-BURNING
PRICE: chimney-sweep-flat
The inspection schedule is fixed. NFPA 211 calls for chimneys, fireplaces and vents to be inspected at least once a year, and the U.S. Fire Administration gives homeowners the same advice. Sweeping depends on what the inspection finds. The CSIA's Level 1 procedure tells technicians to sweep when buildup reaches 1/8 inch or more. Connecticut's consumer protection agency uses the same threshold for removing creosote. Buildup at that level matters because the CSIA describes creosote as highly combustible. Skipping a needed sweep saves one visit and risks a much larger repair. The caveat: burning habits change how fast deposits build up. Wet wood and smoldering fires produce more, so the yearly inspection is what sets your real schedule. See [creosote](/learn/creosote).

## Drop cloths and dust control {#dust-control}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: chimney-sweep-flat
Soot is fine, greasy and hard to get out of carpet, so protecting the room is part of the job, not an extra. The CSIA's procedure has technicians lay a drop cloth before bringing equipment inside. Beyond that, methods differ from company to company, so ask how they do it when you book. Good questions: will the hearth and nearby floor be covered, and will the firebox opening be sealed while brushing? Where does the debris go afterward? If soot does end up on your furniture, the cost of cleaning it is the company's problem to solve. Get that settled before work starts rather than after. The caveat: the CSIA procedure mentions the drop cloth but doesn't spell out dust control beyond it. It is a matter of the company's own practice, so the only protection you have is asking ahead and checking the room before they leave.

## Rotary cleaning vs. brush and rod {#rotary-vs-brush}
TAGS: NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
PRICE: chimney-sweep-flat, creosote-deglazing
There are two common ways to sweep a flue. With the traditional method, a stiff brush sized to the flue is pushed up and down on rods. The rotary method spins flexible whip lines or a rotary head on rods driven by a drill. One rotary kit maker says its whips remove soot and creosote without scratching flues. That is a manufacturer's claim, not an independent test. Either method handles ordinary soot and flaky deposits. The right choice depends on the flue shape, the liner material and how hard the deposits are. Stainless steel liners have their own maker's instructions for which brushes are acceptable, and using the wrong one can damage the liner or void its warranty. Hardened, glazed creosote is a different job, often priced separately as deglazing. The caveat: the method matters less than the result. Ask to see the flue after cleaning, on camera if possible. See [creosote](/learn/creosote) and [flue liners](/learn/flue-liners).

## A sweep is not an inspection {#sweep-vs-inspection}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
PRICE: chimney-sweep-flat, level-1-inspection
Sweeping removes deposits. Inspecting judges whether the chimney is sound, clear and safe to use. NFPA 211's yearly requirement is for inspection, and cleaning is done when it is needed. The CSIA describes the ideal as an annual inspection plus cleaning and repair whenever needed. Its procedure also says a dirty flue has to be swept before the liner can be judged, which is why the two are usually done together. The risk is paying for a sweep that comes with no statement about the flue's condition. You have a clean chimney and no idea whether the liner is cracked. When booking, ask whether the price includes a Level 1 inspection and a written report, or only the cleaning. The caveat: a Level 1 still only covers the readily accessible parts. After a home purchase, a new appliance or a chimney fire, ask about a Level 2. See [chimney inspection](/learn/chimney-inspection).

## What a sweep should show you before leaving {#show-you}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211
At the end of the visit, you should be able to see three things. The first is what came out of the flue. The second is photos or camera footage of the flue and the top of the chimney. The third is a written summary of what was found. The National Chimney Sweep Guild tells homeowners to expect a written report and photos after an inspection. The CSIA's procedure has technicians record the amount and type of deposits and any damaged parts. Connecticut's consumer protection agency adds that photos used to justify repairs should show something that identifies your house. None of this costs the company much, and it gives you a baseline to compare against next year. The caveat: a photo of a crack isn't a diagnosis on its own. Ask what the crack means for safe use and which standard or instruction it relates to.

## The upsell moment, and how to handle it {#upsell}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211, VARIES BY STATE
The most expensive moment of a low-priced sweep is often the one after the brushing, when the technician comes down with bad news. Sometimes the news is real. Either way, you handle it the same way. Ask to see it on the camera or in a photo that shows your house. Ask which standard or manufacturer instruction it breaks. Ask for a written estimate describing the work, the materials and the price. The FTC warns against signing anything under pressure or paying the full amount up front, and says to get several estimates. Connecticut's consumer protection agency advises a second opinion when carbon monoxide or fire is mentioned in a way that feels alarming. The cost of saying yes on the spot can be a liner or crown job you didn't need. The caveat: some findings really do mean you should stop using the fireplace today. Waiting for a second opinion is fine as long as you don't burn in the meantime. Cancellation rights for contracts signed at home vary by state.

## Gas appliance flues still need service {#gas-appliances}
TAGS: APPLIES: GAS APPLIANCES, NATIONAL · MANUFACTURER INSTRUCTIONS
PRICE: gas-fireplace-annual-service
Gas doesn't leave wood soot behind, but its venting can still fail. The manufacturer's manual for Vermont Castings' B-Vent gas fireplaces tells owners to have both the burner and the venting system inspected every year by a qualified service person. It also says to clean or repair the vent if needed. The CPSC urges annual checks of chimneys, flues and vents for leaks and blockages. The CSIA notes that high-efficiency gas appliances produce very wet exhaust, which can make old clay liners flake and block the flue. The consequence of a blocked gas vent is carbon monoxide in the house, not a chimney fire, so it can go unnoticed. Annual gas service is usually priced separately from a wood-flue sweep. The caveat: your appliance's own manual controls, and different models call for different service. Look up yours by model number, and ask whether the technician holds NFI Gas Specialist certification.

## Credentials to ask a sweep about {#credentials}
TAGS: NATIONAL · NFPA 211, VARIES BY STATE
No single national license covers chimney sweeping, so credentials from trade groups are what you can check. Ask about them in this order. First, is the company a National Chimney Sweep Guild member? That is a company credential. Does the technician hold an NCSG individual certification, such as Certified Chimney Professional? Second, does the technician hold National Fireplace Institute certification, and for which fuel: Gas, Wood or Pellet? Third, is the technician a Chimney Safety Institute of America Certified Chimney Sweep? NCSG also tells homeowners to ask for proof of liability and workers' compensation insurance. Hiring someone uninsured can leave you exposed if a worker is hurt on your roof. The caveat: a certificate shows someone passed an exam, not that this particular visit was done well. Whether a state license is also required varies by state.
